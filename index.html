C3 — Stoichiometry

1. Chemical Formulae and Equations (C3.01)

What Is Stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry comes from the Greek words stoicheion (element) and metron (measure). It is "chemical accountancy" at the level of atoms: using formulae, balanced equations and the mole to work out exactly how much of each substance reacts and how much product is made.

  • The mole is the chemist's standard amount of substance; it contains a known number of particles.
  • Industry uses these calculations to run processes safely and economically, with as little waste as possible. This is the aim of the 12 principles of Green Chemistry, which include maximising atom economy.

Formulae of Elements

Every element has its own chemical symbol, shown in the Periodic Table:

  • A single capital letter, e.g. hydrogen H, oxygen O, nitrogen N.
  • Or two letters, e.g. aluminium Al, argon Ar, magnesium Mg. The second letter is always lower case.
  • Some symbols come from the element's Latin name:

Formulae of Compound Ions

  • Simple ions are single atoms that have lost or gained electrons, e.g. Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻.
  • A compound ion is a group of different atoms covalently bonded together that carries an overall charge. Most are negative because the group has gained electrons; negative compound ions containing oxygen are called oxyanions.
  • Ammonium, NH₄⁺, is the common positive compound ion.
  • In diagrams a compound ion is drawn inside square brackets with the charge outside, because the charge is spread over the whole ion.
  • The formula of a compound ion never changes: the carbonate ion is always CO₃²⁻.

Formulae of Simple Covalent Compounds

The combining power of an atom is sometimes called its valency: in a covalent molecule, it is the number of bonds that the atom makes. (The idea is useful for working out formulae, but the term valency itself is not required knowledge.)

  • Groups I–IV: valency = group number.
  • Groups V–VII: valency = 8 − group number.
  • Group VIII: valency = 0 (the noble gases do not normally form compounds).

The cross-over method (Worked Example C3.01, carbon dioxide):

Step 1: Write down the symbols: C and O.

Step 2: Write the valency under each symbol: C = 4, O = 2.

Step 3: Cross over the valencies, so that each becomes the number of atoms of the other element: C₂O₄.

Step 4: Simplify to the lowest whole-number ratio: CO₂.

The method works for molecules with a central atom, e.g. water H₂O (H = 1, O = 2), methane CH₄ (C = 4, H = 1), carbon dioxide CO₂ and ammonia NH₃ (N = 3, H = 1).

Molecular formula from a diagram: count the atoms of each element in the structure. For example (Figure C3.05), one Br atom bonded to three F atoms is BrF₃; one N atom bonded to three H atoms is NH₃; the propene molecule H₂C=CH–CH₃ contains 3 C and 6 H atoms, so it is C₃H₆.

Formulae of Ionic Compounds

  • Ionic compounds have giant ionic lattice structures, so their formula is simply the whole-number ratio of positive to negative ions. This group of ions is one formula unit.
  • The overall structure must be neutral: the positive and negative charges must balance.
  • Example: in magnesium chloride the ions present are Mg²⁺ and 2Cl⁻, with total charges of 2+ and 2−, so the formula is MgCl₂. Mg²⁺ ions combine with Cl⁻ ions in a 1 : 2 ratio, but Na⁺ ions combine with Cl⁻ ions only in a 1 : 1 ratio (NaCl).

Working out the charge on an ion:

  • Metals and hydrogen form positive ions; non-metals form negative ions.
  • Groups I–IV: charge = group number (e.g. Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺).
  • Groups V–VII: charge = 8 − group number (e.g. O²⁻, Cl⁻).
  • Transition elements can have more than one charge; the Roman numeral in the name gives the charge, e.g. copper(I) is Cu⁺, copper(II) is Cu²⁺ and iron(III) is Fe³⁺.

Worked Example C3.02 (cross over the charges):

  • Aluminium oxide: Al has a charge of 3+ and O has a charge of 2−. Cross over: Al₂O₃ (2 × 3+ = 6+ balances 3 × 2− = 6−).
  • Calcium oxide: Ca has a charge of 2+ and O has a charge of 2−. Cross over: Ca₂O₂, then simplify the ratio: CaO.

Compound ions in formulae: treat a compound ion as a single unit. If more than one is needed, put it in brackets with the number outside, because the formula of the ion itself cannot be changed. Brackets are not needed if there is only one compound ion.

Worked Example C3.03:

  • Sodium carbonate: Na⁺ (1+) and CO₃²⁻ (2−). Cross over: Na₂CO₃ (only one carbonate ion, so no brackets).
  • Ammonium sulfate: NH₄⁺ (1+) and SO₄²⁻ (2−). Cross over: (NH₄)₂SO₄.

Word Equations

Some reactions are spectacular (explosives, or the "volcano" reaction in which ammonium dichromate decomposes, giving out heat and light), while others, such as neutralising an acid with an alkali, show no visible change. Every reaction can be summarised by an equation.

  • A word equation links the names of the reactants (the substances that react) with the names of the products (the new substances formed): reactants → products.
  • magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide
  • hydrogen + oxygen → water (a highly exothermic reaction, used in the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell)
  • Energy is not included in an equation, even when a lot is released, because energy is not a chemical substance.

Balanced Symbol Equations

  • Equations become more useful when they are written with chemical formulae.
  • The law of conservation of mass: the total mass of the products is always equal to the total mass of the reactants.
  • During a reaction bonds are broken and new bonds are made, so the atoms are rearranged. No atoms are created or lost, and atoms do not change into atoms of other elements.
  • So a balanced equation has the same number of each type of atom on both sides, e.g. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O has 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms on each side. Where no number is written in front of a formula, one is meant.

Rules for balancing:

  • Write the correct formula for every reactant and product first.
  • Never change a formula (the small numbers) to make an equation balance; formulae are fixed by the bonding in the substance.
  • Balance only by putting multiplying numbers in front of formulae.
  • Count every type of atom on each side at the end to check.

Example, potassium with water (alkali metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen):

  • potassium + water → potassium hydroxide + hydrogen
  • K + H₂O → KOH + H₂ is not balanced: there are 2 H atoms on the left but 3 on the right.
  • On the left, hydrogen occurs only in H₂O, so the hydrogen atoms must come in pairs. Double the KOH, then double the K and the H₂O on the left.
  • 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂ (2 K, 4 H and 2 O atoms on each side).

Worked Example C3.04 (balancing in four steps, magnesium burning in air):

Step 1: Identify the reactants and products: magnesium burns in air (oxygen) to form magnesium oxide.

Step 2: Write the word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

Step 3: Write the formulae: Mg + O₂ → MgO. This is not balanced because oxygen is diatomic: there are two O atoms on the left but only one on the right.

Step 4: Balance the equation: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

State Symbols

State symbol Meaning
(s) solid
(l) liquid
(g) gas
(aq) aqueous solution (dissolved in water)
  • Water produced in a reaction is a liquid, (l), not (aq).
  • Mg(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Mg(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂(g)
  • HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
  • CuSO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + Na₂SO₄(aq), where the (s) shows that a solid precipitate forms.

Ionic Equations

  • When ionic substances react in solution, some of the ions do not change; these are called spectator ions.
  • An ionic equation shows only the ions (and other substances) that change during the reaction.

How to write an ionic equation:

  • Write the balanced symbol equation with state symbols.
  • Split each ionic compound that is (aq) into its separate ions. Do not split solids, water, gases or covalent molecules.
  • Cross out the spectator ions, which appear unchanged on both sides.
  • Write what is left, and check that the atoms and the charges balance.

Neutralisation (acid + alkali):

[H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)] + [Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)] → [Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)] + H₂O(l)

Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectator ions, leaving H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l). This is the ionic equation for all neutralisation reactions between acids and alkalis.

Precipitation (Figure C3.13):

Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2Na⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s) + 2Na⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq)

Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ are spectator ions, leaving Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(s).

2. Relative Masses of Atoms and Molecules (C3.02)

Relative Atomic Mass (Ar)

  • Atoms are extremely light: one hydrogen atom has a mass of 1.7 × 10⁻²⁴ g (0.000 000 000 000 000 000 000 001 7 g). Masses in grams are inconvenient, so atoms are compared on a relative scale.
  • The standard is an atom of the carbon-12 isotope, which is given a mass of exactly 12 units. On this scale a hydrogen atom has a mass of 1, so one carbon atom has the same mass as 12 hydrogen atoms, and one helium atom (Ar = 4) has the same mass as 4 hydrogen atoms.
  • Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the average mass of an atom of an element, taking into account its naturally occurring isotopes (atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons), on the scale where carbon-12 = 12.
  • Most elements are mixtures of isotopes, so most Ar values are not whole numbers. Values are usually rounded to whole numbers, except chlorine, Ar = 35.5 (chlorine has isotopes of mass 35 and 37). Fluorine is unusual in having just one isotope, fluorine-19.
  • An ion has the same relative mass as its atom; the mass of the electrons lost or gained is so small that it is ignored.

Relative Molecular Mass and Relative Formula Mass (Mr)

  • Relative molecular mass (Mr): the sum of the Ar values of all the atoms in one molecule. It is used for covalent substances made of molecules.
  • Relative formula mass (Mr): the sum of the Ar values of all the atoms in one formula unit. It is used for ionic compounds, which are giant structures with no molecules.
  • Ar and Mr are relative values, so they have no units.
  • Examples: Mr(H₂) = 2 × 1 = 2; Mr(H₂O) = (2 × 1) + 16 = 18; Mr(NaCl) = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 (formula unit Na⁺Cl⁻).
Substance Formula Atoms in the formula Mr
Hydrogen H₂ 2 H 2 × 1 = 2
Carbon dioxide CO₂ 1 C, 2 O 12 + (2 × 16) = 44
Calcium carbonate CaCO₃ 1 Ca, 1 C, 3 O 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100
Ammonium sulfate (NH₄)₂SO₄ 2 N, 8 H, 1 S, 4 O (2 × 14) + (8 × 1) + 32 + (4 × 16) = 132
Hydrated magnesium sulfate MgSO₄·7H₂O 1 Mg, 1 S, 4 O, plus 7 H₂O (14 H, 7 O) 24 + 32 + (4 × 16) + (14 × 1) + (7 × 16) = 246
  • A number after a bracket multiplies everything inside it: (NH₄)₂ contains 2 N and 8 H atoms.
  • In a hydrated salt, the number in front of H₂O multiplies the whole water molecule: 7H₂O contains 14 H and 7 O atoms.

Compound Formation and Chemical Formulae

  • Samples of the same compound, however they are made, always contain the same elements combined in the same ratio by mass.

Experiment: making magnesium oxide (Figure C3.16)

  • Weigh an empty crucible with its lid, then reweigh it with a coil of magnesium ribbon inside.
  • Heat the crucible strongly. Lift the lid very carefully from time to time to let air in, but do not let any solid escape as white smoke.
  • Let the crucible cool, then reweigh it. The increase in mass is the mass of oxygen that has combined with the magnesium.

(a) Crucible + lid 8.52
(b) Crucible + lid + magnesium 8.88
(c) Crucible + lid + magnesium oxide 9.12
Mass of magnesium = (b) − (a) 0.36
Mass of magnesium oxide = (c) − (a) 0.60
Mass of oxygen = 0.60 − 0.36 0.24
  • When the class results are plotted as mass of oxygen (y-axis) against mass of magnesium (x-axis), they give a straight line through the origin (Figure C3.17). The masses are always in the same ratio, so a definite compound with a fixed formula is formed.
  • Magnesium oxide always contains 60% magnesium and 40% oxygen by mass. Ammonium nitrate always contains 35% nitrogen, 60% oxygen and 5% hydrogen by mass.

Calculating Reacting Masses

Because atoms are conserved, the balanced equation and the Ar values give the masses that react:

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

(2 × 24) + (2 × 16) → (2 × 24) + (2 × 16)

48 g + 32 g → 80 g

So 48 g of magnesium reacts with 32 g of oxygen to give 80 g of magnesium oxide. Any masses in the same ratio react in the same way.

Worked Example C3.06 (simple proportion): 0.24 g of magnesium reacts with 0.16 g of oxygen to produce 0.40 g of magnesium oxide. How much magnesium oxide is produced by burning 12 g of magnesium?

  • 0.24 g of Mg produces 0.40 g of MgO.
  • So 1 g of Mg produces 0.40 ÷ 0.24 = 1.67 g of MgO.
  • So 12 g of Mg produces 12 × 1.67 = 20 g of MgO.

3. The Mole and the Avogadro Constant (C3.03)

Why Chemists Use the Mole

  • Atoms and molecules are far too small to weigh out or count one at a time, so chemists need a "counting unit". Banks do the same with coins: they weigh them rather than count them, because a fixed number of a particular coin always has the same mass.
  • Chemists count particles by weighing them. For example, concentration (the amount of a substance in a given volume) can be measured in g/dm³.
  • A mass in grams does not directly tell us the number of particles, because atoms of different elements have different masses. So chemists measure amounts in moles.

The Mole, Molar Mass and the Avogadro Constant

  • One mole of a substance has a mass equal to its Ar or Mr in grams, and contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles: atoms, molecules or formula units, depending on the substance.
  • The mass of one mole is the molar mass, measured in g/mol.
  • 6.02 × 10²³ is the Avogadro constant (symbol L), named after the 19th-century Italian chemist Amedeo Avogadro.
  • Example: ethanol has Mr = 46, so 46 g of ethanol is 1 mole and contains 6.02 × 10²³ ethanol molecules.
Substance Formula Ar or Mr Mass of 1 mole Particles in 1 mole
Carbon C 12 12 g 6.02 × 10²³ C atoms
Iron Fe 56 56 g 6.02 × 10²³ Fe atoms
Hydrogen H₂ 2 × 1 = 2 2 g 6.02 × 10²³ H₂ molecules
Oxygen O₂ 2 × 16 = 32 32 g 6.02 × 10²³ O₂ molecules
Water H₂O (2 × 1) + 16 = 18 18 g 6.02 × 10²³ H₂O molecules
Magnesium oxide MgO 24 + 16 = 40 40 g 6.02 × 10²³ MgO formula units
Calcium carbonate CaCO₃ 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100 100 g 6.02 × 10²³ CaCO₃ formula units
Silicon(IV) oxide SiO₂ 28 + (2 × 16) = 60 60 g 6.02 × 10²³ SiO₂ formula units

Mass Calculations

Finding the molar mass of a substance:

Step 1: Write down the formula, e.g. ethanol is C₂H₅OH.

Step 2: Work out Mr. Ethanol contains 2 C atoms (Ar = 12), 6 H atoms (Ar = 1) and 1 O atom (Ar = 16), so Mr = (2 × 12) + (6 × 1) + 16 = 46.

Step 3: Express this in grams per mole: the molar mass of ethanol is 46 g/mol.

The key equation:

amount of substance (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol)

The calculation triangle (Figure C3.20): mass is written at the top, with number of moles and molar mass side by side at the bottom (multiplied together). Cover up the quantity you want:

  • number of moles = mass ÷ molar mass
  • mass = number of moles × molar mass
  • molar mass = mass ÷ number of moles

Worked Example C3.07: How many moles are there in 60 g of sodium hydroxide, NaOH?

  • Mr(NaOH) = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40, so the molar mass is 40 g/mol.
  • number of moles = mass ÷ molar mass = 60 g ÷ 40 g/mol = 1.5 mol

Calculating Reacting Amounts

  • A balanced equation tells us not only what the products are, but also how much product to expect from given amounts of reactants.
  • Fe + S → FeS: 1 mol of iron (56 g) reacts with 1 mol of sulfur (32 g) to give 1 mol of iron(II) sulfide (88 g). The total mass is unchanged (56 + 32 = 88), as the law of conservation of mass requires.
  • The masses of iron and sulfur must always be in the ratio 56 : 32, e.g. 5.6 g Fe + 3.2 g S → 8.8 g FeS.
  • If 5.0 g of sulfur is reacted with 5.6 g of iron, only 3.2 g of the sulfur can react, so 5.0 − 3.2 = 1.8 g of sulfur remains unreacted.

The chemical "bridge" (Figure C3.21), following "up, across, down":

  • Up: change the mass of the substance you know into moles (moles = mass ÷ molar mass).
  • Across: use the mole ratio from the balanced equation to find the moles of the substance you want.
  • Down: change those moles into a mass (mass = moles × molar mass).
  • The bridge works in either direction: from reactant to product, or from product back to reactant.

Worked Example C3.08: What mass of aluminium oxide is produced when 9.2 g of aluminium reacts completely with oxygen gas?

Balanced equation: 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃, so the ratio Al : Al₂O₃ = 4 mol : 2 mol.

Step 1 (up): number of moles of Al = 9.2 g ÷ 27 g/mol = 0.34 mol

Step 2 (across): 4 mol of Al produce 2 mol of Al₂O₃, so 0.34 mol of Al produce 0.17 mol of Al₂O₃.

Step 3 (down): Mr(Al₂O₃) = (2 × 27) + (3 × 16) = 102, so mass of Al₂O₃ = 0.17 mol × 102 g/mol = 17.3 g

  • Read reacting-mass questions carefully and set out the calculation using the balanced equation, so that you can see which substances matter. Always use the balancing numbers: this is the stoichiometry of the equation.
  • Keeping the unrounded number of moles (9.2 ÷ 27 = 0.3407… mol) in your calculator gives 17.4 g, so round only at the end.

Limiting Reactants

  • Reactants are not always mixed in the exact amounts shown by the equation. Sometimes too much of one reactant (an excess) is used on purpose.
  • The limiting reactant is the reactant that is not in excess. It is used up completely, the reaction stops when it runs out, and it decides how much product is formed. The other reactant is left over and is said to be in excess.
  • Figure C3.22 shows 2A + B → A₂B: when all of A has reacted, some B is left over, so A is the limiting reactant.
  • In Worked Example C3.08 an excess of oxygen made sure that all the aluminium reacted, so aluminium was the limiting reactant.

How to find the limiting reactant:

  • Work out the number of moles of each reactant.
  • Divide each by its balancing number (coefficient) in the balanced symbol equation.
  • The smallest number shows the limiting reactant.

Worked Example C3.09: 6.0 g of cobalt(II) carbonate was reacted with 0.08 mol of hydrochloric acid to produce cobalt(II) chloride. Show that the cobalt(II) carbonate was in excess.

CoCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CoCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) (Ar: Co = 59, C = 12, O = 16)

  • Mr(CoCO₃) = 59 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 119, so the molar mass is 119 g/mol.
  • number of moles of CoCO₃ = 6.0 g ÷ 119 g/mol = 0.05 mol
  • 1 mol of CoCO₃ reacts with 2 mol of HCl, so 0.05 mol of CoCO₃ would react with 0.10 mol of HCl.
  • Only 0.08 mol of HCl was present, so the acid is used up first and the cobalt(II) carbonate is in excess.
  • Coefficient check: CoCO₃ gives 0.05 ÷ 1 = 0.05 and HCl gives 0.08 ÷ 2 = 0.04. HCl gives the smaller number, so it is the limiting reactant.

Mole Calculations Involving Gases

  • Weighing a gas is difficult, but measuring its volume is easy.
  • Avogadro's law: equal volumes of any gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of particles.
  • So one mole of any gas occupies the same volume: about 24 dm³ (24 litres) at room temperature and pressure (r.t.p.), which is 25 °C (298 K) and 101.3 kPa (1 atmosphere). This is the molar gas volume.
  • 1 dm³ = 1 litre = 1000 cm³, so the molar gas volume is also 24 000 cm³.
Gas Formula Molar mass (g/mol) Molar gas volume at r.t.p. (dm³/mol) Particles in 1 mole
Hydrogen H₂ 2 24 6.02 × 10²³ molecules
Oxygen O₂ 32 24 6.02 × 10²³ molecules
Carbon dioxide CO₂ 44 24 6.02 × 10²³ molecules
Ethane C₂H₆ 30 24 6.02 × 10²³ molecules

The key equation:

number of moles of gas = volume (dm³) ÷ molar gas volume (24 dm³/mol at r.t.p.)

Worked Example C3.10: If 8 g of sulfur is burnt, what volume of SO₂ is produced?

S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g): 1 mol of S (32 g) reacts with 1 mol of O₂ (24 dm³) to give 1 mol of SO₂ (24 dm³).

  • number of moles of sulfur = 8 g ÷ 32 g/mol = 0.25 mol
  • From the equation, 1 mol of S gives 1 mol of SO₂, so 0.25 mol of S gives 0.25 mol of SO₂.
  • volume of SO₂ = 0.25 mol × 24 dm³/mol = 6 dm³ at r.t.p.

Reactions between gases: gas volumes react and form in the same ratio as the numbers of moles in the equation.

H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)

1 mol (24 dm³) + 1 mol (24 dm³) → 2 mol (48 dm³), which is 1 volume : 1 volume : 2 volumes.

So 20 cm³ of hydrogen reacting with enough chlorine produces 40 cm³ of hydrogen chloride gas.

Concentration in g/dm³

  • Concentration is the amount of a substance (the solute) dissolved in a given volume of solution.
  • concentration (g/dm³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
  • Convert cm³ into dm³ first by dividing by 1000. For example, for 5 g dissolved in 250 cm³ of solution: volume = 250 ÷ 1000 = 0.250 dm³, so concentration = 5 g ÷ 0.250 dm³ = 20 g/dm³.

Green Chemistry

  • The mole and the ratios in which chemicals react are used to decide the conditions for key industrial processes, such as the Haber and Contact processes. These ideas are part of the Green Chemistry approach, set out in 12 principles.
  • The principles include: maximising the amount of the raw materials that ends up in the desired product (measured as atom economy); using renewable raw materials and energy sources; using the least environmentally harmful substances and solvents; using energy-efficient processes; and avoiding the production of waste.

Diatomic molecule A molecule made of two atoms bonded together, e.g. H₂, N₂, O₂, Cl₂

Products The substance(s) produced by a chemical reaction

5. Key Equations

Relative Masses

  1. Mr = sum of the Ar values of all the atoms in the formula (no units)
  2. Percentage of an element by mass = (number of atoms of the element × Ar) ÷ Mr × 100
  3. Reacting masses by proportion: mass of product = mass of reactant used × (known mass of product ÷ known mass of reactant)

The Mole

  1. Amount of substance (mol) = mass (g) ÷ molar mass (g/mol)
  2. Mass (g) = amount (mol) × molar mass (g/mol)
  3. Molar mass (g/mol) = mass (g) ÷ amount (mol), and it is numerically equal to Ar or Mr
  4. Avogadro constant, L = 6.02 × 10²³ particles per mole
  5. Number of particles = amount (mol) × 6.02 × 10²³

Reacting Amounts

  1. The bridge: mass of A ÷ molar mass of A = moles of A; moles of A × (ratio B : A from the equation) = moles of B; moles of B × molar mass of B = mass of B
  2. Limiting reactant: divide the moles of each reactant by its balancing number; the smallest value is the limiting reactant

Gases at r.t.p. (25 °C and 101.3 kPa)

  1. Amount of gas (mol) = volume (dm³) ÷ 24 dm³/mol
  2. Volume of gas (dm³) = amount (mol) × 24 dm³/mol
  3. Amount of gas (mol) = volume (cm³) ÷ 24 000 cm³/mol
  4. 1 dm³ = 1 litre = 1000 cm³
  5. For reacting gases, the ratio of volumes = the ratio of moles in the balanced equation

Concentration

  1. Concentration (g/dm³) = mass of solute (g) ÷ volume of solution (dm³)
  2. Volume (dm³) = volume (cm³) ÷ 1000

Using some of your examples, compare this method to the cross-over method described earlier in this chapter.

Reflection — Balancing Equations and Relative Masses

Do you have a successful strategy to help you balance equations? What aspects do you find easy or difficult? Could you explain how to calculate relative molecular or formula masses to someone else?

Answer:

  • A strategy for balancing: write the correct formulae first and never change them; count the atoms of each element on both sides (a small tally table helps); balance the elements that appear in only one substance on each side first, and leave oxygen, hydrogen and any element appearing on its own (such as O₂ or H₂) until last; treat a compound ion such as SO₄ as a single unit if it appears unchanged on both sides; finally, recount every atom.
  • Easy and difficult aspects: simple equations such as 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO are straightforward. It is harder when an element appears in several substances, as in 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂, or when a diatomic element means that a product has to be doubled and the other side rebalanced.
  • Explaining Mr: write the formula; count the atoms of each element (a number after a bracket multiplies everything inside it); multiply the number of atoms of each element by its Ar; add up the results. For example, Mr(Cu(NO₃)₂) = 64 + 2 × [14 + (3 × 16)] = 188. Mr has no units.

Getting to grips with the mole: the mole links the number of particles of a chemical to the relative mass of that chemical; in simple terms, it is the chemist's way of describing the amount of substance present. Answer: A mole is the chemist's unit for the amount of a substance. One mole of any substance contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant), which may be atoms, molecules or formula units, and it has a mass equal to the Ar or Mr of the substance in grams (its molar mass). Using Ar: Li = 7 and O = 16:

  • 1 mol of lithium is 6.02 × 10²³ Li atoms, with a mass of 7 g.
  • 1 mol of oxygen gas is 6.02 × 10²³ O₂ molecules, with a mass of 2 × 16 = 32 g.
  • 1 mol of lithium oxide is 6.02 × 10²³ Li₂O formula units, with a mass of (2 × 7) + 16 = 30 g.

Activity C3.02 — Question 3

Answer: The ratio 4 : 1 : 2 is fixed whatever amounts are used. Every 4 mol of Li gives 2 mol of Li₂O, so the moles of Li₂O are always half the moles of Li.

  • 400 mol of Li: moles of Li₂O = 400 × 2 ÷ 4 = 200 mol, using 400 ÷ 4 = 100 mol of O₂.

Activity C3.02 — Question 4

Your explanation should include how an understanding of the number of moles allows masses to be deduced by use of the equation: number of moles = mass (g) ÷ molar mass.

Answer: Rearranging gives mass = number of moles × molar mass, so once the numbers of moles are known from the equation, the masses can be calculated. Using Ar: Li = 7 and O = 16, the molar masses are Li = 7 g/mol, O₂ = 32 g/mol and Li₂O = 30 g/mol.

  • 4 mol of Li = 4 × 7 = 28 g; 1 mol of O₂ = 32 g; 2 mol of Li₂O = 2 × 30 = 60 g. The masses balance (28 g + 32 g = 60 g), as the law of conservation of mass requires.
  • Example: what mass of Li₂O is formed from 2.8 g of lithium? Moles of Li = 2.8 g ÷ 7 g/mol = 0.40 mol; moles of Li₂O = 0.40 ÷ 2 = 0.20 mol; mass of Li₂O = 0.20 mol × 30 g/mol = 6.0 g.

Activity C3.02 — Question 5

You might want to explain why the number of moles in the reactants and products does not have to be the same. In this case, for example, there are five moles of reactants but two moles of products.

Answer: A chemical reaction conserves atoms (and therefore mass), not the number of particles. In 4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O there are 4 Li atoms and 2 O atoms on each side, and the mass is 60 g on each side. However, the atoms are regrouped: 4 separate Li atoms and 1 O₂ molecule (5 particles) become 2 Li₂O formula units, each containing 3 atoms. Fewer, larger particles are formed, so 5 mol of reactants give only 2 mol of product.

The mole and a green approach to industrial chemistry: the mole and the ratios in which chemicals react are very important in deciding the conditions used in key industrial processes such as the Haber and Contact processes. Project C3.01 — Podcast Question 1

Following your discussion, work in groups to prepare a podcast for the class explaining the importance of the green approach to industrial chemistry. Why is it important to have a balanced symbol equation for your reaction?

Answer:

  • A balanced symbol equation shows the correct formulae of all the reactants and products, including any by-products that will have to be dealt with.
  • It gives the mole ratio (the stoichiometry) of the reaction. Together with molar masses, this lets chemists calculate the exact masses (or gas volumes) of raw materials needed and the maximum mass of product that can be made.
  • Using exactly the right amounts avoids wasting raw materials and energy, and it allows the atom economy of the process to be worked out and improved.
  • It obeys the law of conservation of mass, so every atom can be accounted for.

Project C3.01 — Podcast Question 2

Why is it important to consider limiting reactants and those in excess in an industrial reaction?

Answer:

  • The limiting reactant decides the maximum amount of product that can be made; adding more of the other reactant will not make any more product.
  • Any reactant in excess is left over at the end. It is wasted unless it is separated and recycled, which costs money and energy, and it may contaminate the product or cause pollution.
  • Sometimes a cheap reactant (such as oxygen from the air) is deliberately used in excess to make sure that all of a more expensive reactant is used up, as when aluminium is reacted with excess oxygen.
  • Calculating the correct amounts keeps waste and costs to a minimum, which supports the Green Chemistry principles of preventing waste and maximising atom economy.

Practice Question 1

Every atom has its own atomic mass, Ar. These are added together to find the relative formula mass, Mr, of a substance. The Mr value for calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, is 100. Identify what mass of carbon is present in 100 g of calcium carbonate. [1]

A: 12 g

B: 60 g

C: 48 g

D: 36 g

Answer: A, 12 g. [1]

Each formula unit of CaCO₃ contains one carbon atom (Ar = 12), and Mr(CaCO₃) = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = 100. So 100 g of calcium carbonate (1 mol) contains 1 mol of carbon atoms, which is 12 g: mass of carbon = (12 ÷ 100) × 100 g = 12 g.

B (60 g) is the mass of the carbonate group, CO₃ (12 + 48); C (48 g) is the mass of the oxygen (3 × 16); D (36 g) wrongly counts three carbon atoms.

7. Exam Focus Priorities (Ranked by Frequency)

  1. Moles from masses — amount (mol) = mass ÷ molar mass and its rearrangements; working out molar masses in g/mol; this appears in almost every calculation question.
  2. Writing and balancing symbol equations — correct formulae first, then numbers in front only; state symbols are often asked for, and water formed is (l).
  3. Relative formula mass (Mr) — adding Ar values, taking care with brackets and water of crystallisation; defining Ar, Mr and the difference between molecular mass and formula mass.
  4. Reacting masses — by simple proportion, or by the bridge (mass → moles → mole ratio → moles → mass), always using the balancing numbers.
  5. Gas volume calculations — 24 dm³ (24 000 cm³) per mole at r.t.p.; converting cm³ and dm³; volume ratios for reacting gases, including the gas left in excess and ignoring liquid water.
  6. Formulae of ionic compounds — using ion charges so that the charges balance; brackets around compound ions, e.g. (NH₄)₂SO₄, Mg(NO₃)₂.
  7. Ionic equations — splitting aqueous ionic compounds into ions, removing spectator ions; H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) and precipitation reactions.
  8. Limiting reactant and excess — moles ÷ balancing number, smallest value is limiting; the limiting reactant fixes the amount of product.
  9. Formulae from structures — counting atoms in diagrams to give a molecular formula; recognising ionic lattices (formula unit) versus covalent molecules.
  10. Definitions — mole, Avogadro constant (6.02 × 10²³), molar mass, molar gas volume, r.t.p., relative atomic and formula mass.
  11. Concentration in g/dm³ — mass ÷ volume in dm³, converting cm³ by dividing by 1000.
  12. Fixed composition of compounds — the magnesium oxide experiment: finding the mass of oxygen by difference; a straight-line graph shows a fixed ratio by mass.

8. Quick Revision Card

  • Symbols: one capital letter, with any second letter lower case; Latin-based symbols include Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au, Pb.
  • Elements: metals, giant covalent elements and noble gases are written as just the symbol; simple molecules are H₂, N₂, O₂, Cl₂, P₄, S₈.
  • Covalent formulae: valency is the group number for Groups I–IV and 8 − group number for Groups V–VII; cross over and simplify (C₂O₄ → CO₂).
  • Ionic formulae: charges must balance; put compound ions in brackets when more than one is needed: Al₂O₃, CaO, Na₂CO₃, (NH₄)₂SO₄, Mg(NO₃)₂.
  • Balancing: never change a formula, only add numbers in front; atoms are rearranged, so mass is conserved.
  • State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq); water formed in a reaction is (l).
  • Ionic equations: cancel the spectator ions; neutralisation is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
  • Ar is measured relative to carbon-12 = 12; chlorine is 35.5 because of its isotopes; Mr = sum of the Ar values, with no units.
  • A compound always has the same composition by mass (magnesium oxide is 60% Mg and 40% O).
  • 1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro constant, L) = Mr in grams (molar mass, g/mol).
  • moles = mass ÷ molar mass; mass = moles × molar mass.
  • Bridge: mass → moles → use the ratio from the equation → moles → mass.
  • Limiting reactant: divide the moles of each reactant by its balancing number; the smallest is limiting and decides the amount of product.
  • Gases: 1 mol of any gas = 24 dm³ = 24 000 cm³ at r.t.p. (25 °C, 101.3 kPa); moles = volume (dm³) ÷ 24.
  • Reacting gases: volumes are in the same ratio as moles (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl is 1 : 1 : 2).
  • Concentration (g/dm³) = mass (g) ÷ volume (dm³); 1 dm³ = 1 litre = 1000 cm³.
